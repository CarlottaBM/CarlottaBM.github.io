Experimental Work

Tools for asking physical questions

My experimental work spans detector characterization, vacuum and gas-loading systems, and quantum-optics protocols.

Across these projects, I am interested in the full path from apparatus and calibration to analysis and a defensible physical result.

Detection and characterization in the ARPA-E project

At MIT, I have worked on an ARPA-E project testing nuclear-anomaly claims in gas-loaded metal-hydrogen systems. I helped design and assemble vacuum and gas-loading setups, integrate components, and leak test the completed platform.

For particle detection, I calibrated Eljen liquid-scintillation neutron detectors with a Cf-252 source and established reproducible background baselines. This work included count-rate distributions, background separation, and uncertainty analysis; a public output is the Eljen background characterization notebook.

I also use CR-39 track detectors to study charged-particle signatures. I developed workflows for detector-image preprocessing, track geometry, and physical inference; the machine-learning component is described on my Machine Learning page. I presented work from the wider project at the ARPA-E Energy Innovation Summit.

Quantum optics and E91 key distribution

In MIT’s Quantum Engineering Platforms course, I built a project based on the E91 quantum key-distribution protocol. The work translated an entanglement-based security protocol into an experimental workflow involving state preparation, measurement-basis choices, correlation analysis, and key generation.

This project gave me experience connecting an ideal quantum-information protocol to the practical constraints of a quantum-optics platform: calibration, finite data, imperfect measurements, and the question of which correlations can actually be trusted.
