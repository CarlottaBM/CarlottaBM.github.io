Quantum & Analogue Simulation

When does quantum structure change how a system moves?

I use simulation to understand transport, coupling, resonance, and the boundary between classical and quantum behavior.

I am interested in comparing quantum systems with classical analogues: identifying which behavior depends on genuinely quantum structure and which can be reproduced by a simpler model.

Delocalization as a resource

My bachelor’s thesis compared quantum and classical descriptions of transport. I studied how a state spread across several sites can support motion through an energy or graph landscape, and how this idea appears in both quantum annealing and exciton diffusion in light-harvesting systems.

Quantum walks provided a common language for comparing these problems. They let me explore how coherence, delocalization, and environmental coupling affect transport—and when a classical random walk captures the same behavior.

This “resource” perspective connects back to quantum information: rather than treating quantumness as a binary property, it asks which physical feature is useful for a particular task. The full analysis is available in my undergraduate thesis.

Extending the comparison

I am continuing this line of thinking through coupled-oscillator analogue models of resonance, driven response, mode coupling, and energy transfer. These systems offer an intuitive way to test how much of an apparently quantum dynamical effect follows from wave behavior alone.

The broader question

Across these models, I am asking the same question: what can simulation reveal about a physical system that we could not learn efficiently from a classical description?
